## Resumo disponível

Future readers will struggle to fathom how fast it happened. Overnight, academic leaders committed hundreds of buildings, thousands of faculty lines, and billions of research dollars to the reinvigorated study of AI.1 It was too little, too late, but also too much, too fast: a panicked response to technological achievements which many admired but few understood. Specifically, it was part of a culture-wide response to ChatGPT, whose weird charisma had caught the world’s attention and convinced it of the power of large language models (LLMs). Soon enough, this first tide of exuberance receded, but a sea change was already complete.2 We suddenly lived in a world remade by this technology, or by the dreams it inspired among the powerful.The essays collected here are best understood as products of this heady moment, but written by scholars who know how to keep their heads. While LLMs may have caught some scholars off guard, these three—having long studied performance in relation to advanced technology—could afford to take a more deliberate approach. Equipped with deeper histories and durable theories, they neither swallowed the intoxicating new nor spat it out but, ruminating, reached for older ideas: decolonial theories of orature (Eacho), pre-digital notions of virtuality (Muse), and nineteenth-century histories of theater as a technical apparatus (Worthen). The point in dusting off such notions was to answer pressing questions: What must theater and performance studies become in this era of technocapitalism? What new projects should the field take onboard? What old ballast should we toss? And at the root of it all, as always, the one nagging question: What good is theater, theater history, or performance theory today?On the evidence of his essay here, performance matters to Eacho for what it offers to the study of social experience. What began as theories of theatrical aesthetics were reformulated by performance studies as theories of social life, and in that form they have roamed as far as Silicon Valley and the corporate boardroom. Right now, the tech industry has welcomed one of these theories—what Eacho calls “the cult of the immediate” or “immediacy’s fantastic idyll”—because it helps them pursue their own ends, such as the deeper integration of technical systems into our lives. Tech executives invoke fantasies of liveness, radical presence, and flow—familiar to anyone from our field—as a vision of how to access AI systems: invisibly, via voice-user interfaces like ChatGPT’s Voice Mode, Meta/Ray-Ban’s smart glasses, or one of the many AI pendants and pins now coming to market. In doing so, tech companies make a cruelly optimistic argument: that “immediacy” will be achieved through ever-more-pervasive regimes of technological mediation.3Implicit in Eacho’s essay is a demand upon our field: that we study corporate behavior and rhetoric with all the theoretical rigor and aesthetic attention we bring to more traditional objects, like dramatic texts, acting traditions, and dance practices. This is not a new demand but reemerges now as the repressed so inevitably does. Nearly a quarter-century ago, in his book Perform Or Else, Jon McKenzie named “organizational performance” as one of three paradigms (alongside “cultural performance” and “technological performance”) to be studied and theorized by our field.4 This has not yet resulted in any flowering of scholarship on corporate practices and theories of performance, but we might understand Eacho’s work as an heir to this neglected tradition. Back then, McKenzie was thinking about the post-1960 rise of “performance management” as a theory of corporate operations and as an “ars poetica of organizational practice.”5 Not only did the theorists of “performance management” draw consciously on the performing arts for models of organizational pleasure and power, but their field also emerged in tandem with performance studies itself and developed along parallel lines.6 Like all doppelgängers, “performance management” is both entrancing and repellent, which may account for the lack of broader uptake by the field. Eacho’s essay demonstrates the benefits of uptake, however belated.Such work is sorely needed now, when corporate actors often come dressed in the stolen valor of art. As Fred Turner has shown, the economy is now ruled by an industry (Big Tech) that styles itself as an artful, bohemian counterculture; and, as Shannon Steen argues, society (politics, economics, education) is now ordered by a “creativity complex” that threatens to reabsorb the arts like maker culture’s vanishing twin.7 In such a moment, performance scholars would be wise to reconsider McKenzie’s book and the agenda it proposed. If we were to look more deeply, as McKenzie advised, into the circus mirror of corporate performance theories, we would uncover further layers of what Eacho exposes here: a technocapitalist hunger for some insights our field supplies, and an aversion or blindness to others. According to Eacho, we should unlearn our attachment to the former (especially what he calls a “media-denying performance theory,” which Big Tech invokes when marketing the “immediacy” their intrusive media make possible) and devote ourselves instead to the latter (especially critical perspectives on “media-intensive performance[s] of the present”). Scholars will and should debate the details of strategy, but they should not delay in devoting more energy to such questions.Does this mean that we all have to become close-readers of press releases, dramaturges of product launches, or performance theorists of interface design? Given my own work in this vein (e.g., on the theatricality of corporate “conversation design”), you might expect me to answer yes.8 But my career, like Eacho’s, suggests otherwise. After all, my scholarship on corporate tech relies crucially, if covertly, on earlier research into theater where actors took machines for scene partners, treating communications technologies as fellow actors in a theatrical or filmic scene.9 And, although Eacho never alludes to it here, his response to corporate discourse on AI is clearly shaped by his monograph-in-progress on the history of artists trying to automate theater, dance, and other performing arts. In other words, while he applies performance theory to AI and insists on studying its “performative” aesthetics, he does so with a knowledge of performance history and with particular artistic forebears in mind. He sees what he sees in corporate marketing of AI, in other words, because of his knowledge of automated performance before AI.There is a key lesson here, not only for individual scholars but for the field as a whole: No bid for AI-relevance can ignore its foundations in “irrelevant” expertise, for instance about performance. Already, the barriers to building and sharing such expertise are growing, with scant humanities research funds now rerouted toward AI-focused work.10 One extreme instance of the trend was recently described by Sonja Drimmer, a scholar of medieval art and literature at UMass-Amherst: Whereas faculty in the UMass College of Humanities and Fine Arts now receive only $300 in annual conference-going funds, they are eligible for $20,000 research grants if they collaborate with the College of Computer and Information Science.11 Such an unbalanced system may work for a while, as scholars build fundable research agendas on no-longer-fundable foundations; but while this strategy will work for some, and for some time, I fear that it will ultimately fail for want of the expertise on which it was based. Like LLMs themselves, which deskill human writing and pollute the corpus of text on which LLMs continue to be trained, AI-relevant research also risks eroding its own foundations in now unfundable and “irrelevant” research.The other essays in this cluster offer further models of how to prevent such erosion. Neither deals extensively with corporate tech, and only Worthen deals explicitly with LLMs. Instead, both respond to the pressures of this moment in more oblique ways: (1) by appealing to ideas (virtuality for Muse, technicity for Worthen) that precede the era of digital computation, (2) by treating the theater as a site for teaching and studying those ideas, and (3) by demonstrating that new technologies rely upon such ideas, and are therefore neither new nor entirely technological. Those interested in cutting-edge technologies, Muse and Worthen both argue, can therefore benefit from studying theater, including theater made long ago—not only because “theater is a technology” (an idea they both assert) but also because technology is theatrical (an idea they only imply).Of the two, it is Worthen’s essay that argues most extensively that “theater is a technology.” (This should not be surprising, given the similar arguments pursued in his recent book.)12 He pursues this argument not by theoretical fiat but by historical argument. Over the last couple of centuries, Western theater has turned more and more into a “technologically administered space,” he notes. Human actors have remained central, but they are increasingly “suspended” in an apparatus of scenery, lighting, sound, projections, and so forth—all typically controlled by software systems. This makes theater a productive and “engaging place to explore the representation of the technological interface”—not only when a play takes technology as its subject, and not only when a play disrupts or defamiliarizes its apparatus, but even when it invites “mindless” engagement with technology. (“Mindlessness” is a term of art from human-computer interaction, where it refers to people’s ability to ignore the interfaces through which they access computational systems.)13 Importantly, such an argument broadens what theater is relevant to a study of technoculture, and thus it provides a rationale for supporting theater practice and theater scholarship.Whereas Worthen has much say about theater as technology, Muse sheds more light on the obverse idea: that technology is theatrical. Nearly thirty-five years ago, Brenda Laurel (a theater studies PhD turned interface designer) did two things within a year of one another: She founded a virtual reality (VR) company called Telepresence Research, and she published a book called Computers as Theatre.14 In that book, she argued that interfaces (like theatrical stages) are primarily places to represent action—and at Telepresence Research and other ventures, she tried to put that theory into action. Muse’s essay might be read as a response, decades later, to this moment in Laurel’s career—or to that moment in the history of theater studies and VR.Through a metatheatrical reading of Jennifer Haley’s The Nether—a play in part about a futuristic VR platform—Muse suggests both that theater has always been virtual, and that VR relies on habits of mind first forged in the theater. As Muse points out, for a show about advanced technology, The Nether is notable for using very little. Productions of it have been “technologically administered” in all the ways Worthen describes, but their main “technologies” for staging VR have been the ones that theater has used for millennia: human actors lend their bodies to fictional avatars, creating in the process those chimeras known as characters. Haley’s twist on that old theatrical trick, Muse points out, is to double it, casting actors both as characters and as those characters’ VR avatars. Through such an estranging amplification of theater’s representational logic, Muse argues, Haley is able to imply the following arguments about VR: (1) that it relies on weird conflations of self and other, “real” and “unreal,” embodied experience and abstract invention, (2) that these have always been the stock-in-trade of theater, and (3) that theater is therefore a crucial resource for understanding VR.This suggests a broader argument than the one Muse makes explicit in this essay. He presents theater as a repertoire of tactics for understanding and managing unruly conflations of self and other, body and avatar. I would add: theater also teaches how to attach feelings and judgments to such conflations. Haley wants to attach moral queasiness and confusion to them, but this is hardly inevitable. A tech executive could also find in theater ways of cultivating pleasure and ease, which of course brings us back to Eacho’s arguments and to the tech industry’s partial, distorting uptake of performance theory. This is the dialectical bind in which we currently find ourselves, as scholars of performance and of its connection with advanced technologies. We find obvious the “relevance” of what we know to the well-funded and prestigious world of advanced technologies, and so perhaps we feel called to shore up our field (or promote ourselves) by explaining that relevance to others—but we are always at risk of being misheard by people who, after all, can take what they want, discard the rest, and go their merry way.“The similarity between chatbots and dramatic characters prompts reevaluation in both directions,” Muse writes in a recent essay on AI-generated drama.15 Of course, given the value society currently attributes to each of those things, one of those “directions” is easier and less surprising. People presume that we, in the School of Arts and Sciences, will be interested in developments from the School of Engineering. We are expected, as a matter of our continued world citizenship, to reevaluate our work in light of what is happening over there. But we have limited power to demand that anything flow in the other direction. So, what is a scholar to do? Do we dissent and critique from afar? Do we engage and live like parasites off resources meant for others? Or do we try to find ways, within our limited means, to provoke “reevaluation” in the other direction?I sense a lot of confusion and disgust amongst humanists at academic leaders’ new spending spree on AI. (Too much, too late.) I also know that, to the extent that AI researchers choose to work at universities, they forego the money, prestige, and computing power they could have in industry. They might therefore be better allies than we are prone to believe—focused on what can be done at smaller scales, with a longer view and a broader sense of what is valuable. With little enough, soon enough, what might be possible?
