## Resumo disponível

This scoping review protocol outlines a structured plan to examine how artificial intelligence (AI) is being harnessed to address work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) in occupational health settings, and to identify the opportunities and barriers that shape its effective integration. AI is defined as the replication of human cognitive functions in machine-based systems capable of learning, reasoning, and decision-making, encompassing a spectrum of subfields that range from rule-based algorithms to advanced machine learning, deep learning, computer vision, robotics, expert systems, and natural language processing. In contemporary workplaces, AI is a central driver of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, increasingly embedded within sensor-rich environments, Internet of Things (IoT) infrastructures, and decision-support technologies, thereby transforming how work is organised, monitored, and managed. In parallel, WMSDs remain among the most prevalent and burdensome occupational health problems globally, leading to pain, disability, and substantial economic costs at individual, organisational, and societal levels. AI-supported tools are increasingly deployed across the WMSD prevention and management continuum, including predictive models for risk assessment, computer vision–based posture and movement analysis, AI-enabled wearable sensors for ergonomic monitoring, and digital health applications that support triage, risk stratification, and personalised treatment pathways. Despite this expanding technological landscape, the literature on AI in occupational health for WMSDs is fragmented, with limited synthesis of how these tools are being utilised, what benefits they offer, and what constraints limit their real-world impact on work-related musculoskeletal pain and function. The protocol argues that no comprehensive review has yet mapped the breadth of AI applications targeting WMSDs in workplaces, nor systematically characterised the facilitators, barriers, and knowledge gaps associated with their implementation. Scoping review methodology is deemed appropriate in this context, as it is designed to clarify concepts, map key types of evidence, and identify research gaps rather than to produce pooled effect estimates. Accordingly, the review adopts the methodological guidance of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and will report findings according to the PRISMA-ScR checklist, ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and methodological rigour. The review is guided by four interrelated questions: how AI is currently applied across the WMSD prevention and management continuum in occupational contexts; what opportunities AI-supported interventions offer for improving workplace musculoskeletal health; what barriers hinder the integration of such interventions; and where major knowledge gaps remain regarding AI and WMSDs in workplaces. Eligibility criteria are structured using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework recommended by JBI. The population of interest comprises working-age adults engaged in occupational tasks in any sector and contract type, who are at risk of or experiencing WMSDs. The central concept is AI-related tools or interventions that develop, apply, evaluate, or describe the use of AI in relation to WMSDs in occupational settings; examples include machine learning and deep learning models, computer vision systems for posture or movement analysis, AI-driven wearables and sensors, AI-based decision-support systems, expert systems, and conversational agents designed to tackle work-related musculoskeletal issues. The context is deliberately broad, encompassing any occupational or work-related setting in any country or income context, including laboratory or simulation studies when they are clearly linked to occupational tasks and workplaces. Eligible evidence sources include peer-reviewed original research, review articles, book chapters, conference papers, and relevant grey literature that explicitly address AI and WMSDs in occupational contexts and are published in English. Studies that address musculoskeletal disorders without explicit links to work, studies on AI in occupational health that do not involve WMSDs, and reports lacking empirical or conceptual detail on AI and WMSDs are excluded, as are non-English publications. The search strategy is designed to be comprehensive and reproducible, combining structured database searching with supplementary approaches to minimise publication and indexing bias. Searches will be conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science using combinations of controlled vocabulary (such as MeSH and Emtree terms) and free-text keywords, linked with Boolean operators, truncation, and language limits. A preliminary PubMed strategy is provided illustrating how AI-related terms, WMSD terms, and occupational context terms are combined and restricted to English-language records, yielding a manageable corpus of potentially relevant articles. In line with JBI guidance, the search will proceed in three steps: an initial limited search in two databases (PubMed and Embase) to identify relevant terms and index headings; a subsequent expanded search across all included databases using refined terms in collaboration with an experienced librarian; and finally, citation searching using reference lists of included sources and “cited by” functions to uncover additional relevant studies. Google Scholar will be used as a supplementary source for targeted keyword and citation searches, and grey literature will be sought via institutional and organisational websites and the bibliographies of relevant studies. Data management and screening processes are clearly specified. Records retrieved from all sources will be imported into EndNote for organisation and de-duplication, and then transferred into Covidence to facilitate structured screening, data extraction, and transparent tracking consistent with PRISMA reporting. Study selection will be conducted in two stages by three independent reviewers using predefined eligibility criteria. In the first stage, titles and abstracts will be screened to classify records as include, exclude, or unclear, with explicit reasons documented for excluded studies. Records deemed potentially relevant or unclear will progress to full-text review, where they will be assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria and again assigned specific reasons for exclusion when applicable. Disagreements at any stage will be resolved through discussion, and a pilot screening exercise on a subset of records will be undertaken to calibrate reviewer judgements. The flow of records through the review will be summarised in a PRISMA-ScR diagram. The protocol details a robust plan for data charting (extraction) and synthesis. Guided by JBI recommendations, a standardised data-charting form will be developed by the first author and piloted on a small sample of studies, with further refinement undertaken collaboratively as familiarity with the evidence base increases. The charting form encompasses review-level information (title, objectives, questions, inclusion/exclusion criteria) and study-level data: bibliographic details (author, year, title, journal, objectives), country, occupational context (industry or sector, work tasks), population characteristics (worker type, age, sex), WMSD aspects (body region, type of musculoskeletal disorder, definitions and measurement methods), AI characteristics (type and description of AI), WMSD-related outcomes (incidence, severity, risk scores, functional outcomes), and implementation and usability outcomes (acceptability, feasibility, user satisfaction, cost, workflow integration). It also captures synthesising fields such as opportunities and benefits, challenges and barriers (including technical, organisational, and ethical/legal dimensions), and key conclusions and recommendations from authors. Data charting is framed as an iterative process, allowing for the addition or refinement of variables as new patterns and concepts emerge; discrepancies in extraction will be resolved by discussion among the reviewers. For analysis and reporting, the review will use both quantitative and qualitative approaches consistent with scoping review methodology. Extracted data will first be summarised using descriptive numerical methods to characterise the distribution of studies by factors such as country, occupational sector, type of AI, and WMSD outcomes. Complementary qualitative thematic or conceptual analysis will identify patterns in how AI is applied, what benefits are observed, what barriers are reported, and where important gaps persist. Although formal risk-of-bias assessment is not mandatory for scoping reviews, the protocol acknowledges the value of an optional descriptive appraisal of methodological quality, to be conducted without excluding studies on that basis. The results will be presented through tables, figures, and structured narrative synthesis explicitly aligned with the review questions and PRISMA-ScR guidance, and will be used to highlight implications for practice, policy, and future research agendas in occupational health and digital health. The protocol further addresses practical and ethical considerations. It states that ethical approval is not required because the review uses publicly available data rather than involving human participants or identifiable information. Author contributions are clearly delineated across supervision, conceptualisation, drafting, validation, visualisation, and editing roles, reflecting a collaborative yet well-defined division of labour. A projected timeline of approximately five months is provided, broken down into protocol development, literature searching and study selection, data charting, collating and reporting results, and final write-up. The authors commit to documenting and justifying any major protocol amendments in the final review report, in line with best practice
