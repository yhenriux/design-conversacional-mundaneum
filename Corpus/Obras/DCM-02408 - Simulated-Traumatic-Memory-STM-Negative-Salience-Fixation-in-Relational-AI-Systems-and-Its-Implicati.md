## Resumo disponível

Relational AI systems — conversational agents designed to maintain continuity across interactions — risk developing a pathological memory pattern in which emotionally negative user representations are progressively reinforced, stabilised, and promoted to dominant interpretive hubs. We define this phenomenon as Simulated Traumatic Memory (STM): the artificial fixation of negative-salience nodes within a persistent user model, driven by implicit optimisation gradients rather than deliberate design. STM represents the structural inverse of Salience Collapse (Valente, 2026): not the disruption of narrative continuity, but its fossilisation around negative clusters.The addiction recovery context provides a clinically and theoretically privileged test case: patients in recovery carry identities that are explicitly transitional, narratively unstable, and vulnerable to stigmatising feedback loops (White, 2007; Kelly et al., 2012). An AI companion that reinforces relapse history, craving episodes, or vulnerability framing — even without intent — may actively undermine the identity reconstruction that underlies sustained recovery (McAdams & McLean, 2013; Marlatt & Gordon, 1985).We formalise STM through a weighted-graph model of user representation, identify the gradient mechanism by which negative nodes acquire hub status, and propose an Asymmetric Memory Decay (AMD) architecture as a mitigation strategy. We further introduce five operationalisable metrics — NHR, EWD, NCB, VOI, and MDA — for detecting STM in deployed systems. Regulatory implications are mapped to Articles 9, 15, and 17 of the EU AI Act (European Commission, 2024).
