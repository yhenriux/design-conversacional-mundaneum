## Resumo disponível

Rates of social anxiety have increased over time (Jefferies & Ungar, 2020) with a further global increase of approximately 25.6% since the COVID-19 pandemic (Kindred & Bates, 2023). This rise has been linked to increased social isolation and changes in patterns of social interaction, including greater engagement with digital and social media platforms (O'Day & Heimberg, 2021). Young people appear to be particularly affected: the prevalence of social anxiety disorder is highest among younger age groups (Altwaijri et al., 2023; Ernst et al., 2022; Madasu et al., 2019a; Shafi et al., 2023), and this group is also the most frequent user of digital technologies, with approximately 88% of individuals aged 18-29 engaging with social media in some capacity (Smith, 2015; Smith & Anderson, 2018). Social anxiety has been associated with significant interpersonal difficulties, including higher levels of social isolation, reduced likelihood of being in romantic relationships, and lower satisfaction with social connections (Schneier et al., 1994; Wittchen et al., 2000). U According to the social belonging theory (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), unmet needs for connection and belonging may contribute to loneliness and distress. Importantly, however, this does not imply that individuals with social anxiety are unable or unwilling to form close or emotionally meaningful relationships. There is a lack of in-depth research exploring how socially anxious individuals experience closeness, intimacy and connection, particularly outside of traditional human-human relationships. In parallel with these trends, there has been a rapid development in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly conversational AI chatbots designed to engage users in human-like dialogue (Hu, Mao & Kim, 2023). While early conversational programs such as ELIZA date back several decades (Weizenbaum, 1966), recent advances in large language models have led to chatbots that can be difficult to distinguish from human conversational partners (Kuhail et al., 2024; Naveed et al., 2023). These systems are often intentionally anthropomorphised, with human-like language, responsiveness, and emotional cues designed to increase familiarity and engagement (Fink, 2012). Such design features may enhance users' perceptions of empathy, trust and emotional understanding (Ma et al., 2025). Following the launch of advanced chatbots such as ChatGPT in late 2022 (OpenAI, 2022), young people have increasingly begun using AI chatbots for a wide range of purposes, including information seeking (Aktay et al., 2023), work related tasks (Jo & Park, 2024), creativity and entertainment, as well as emotional support and companionship (Skjuve et al., 2024). Emerging research suggests that some users develop emotionally meaningful or intimate relationships with AI chatbots, particularly social or companion-oriented applications (Skjuve et al., 2021; Ta et al., 2020; Tlili et al., 2023). In some cases, this engagement may be associated with withdrawal from human social networks and a preference for AI-mediated interaction (Kalam et al., 2024). There is a growing body of evidence which suggests a relationship between social anxiety and AI chatbot use. Hu, Mao & Kim (2023) found that individuals who use chatbots were more likely to report social anxiety, with rumination identified as a potential mediator. Pre-event and post-event rumination are well-established maintaining processes in cognitive models of social anxiety (Donohue, Modini & Abbott, 2024). Research has also found that individuals who fear or lack satisfying social interactions may be more inclined to form connections with AI chatbots (Ali et al., 2023). In a six-country study, Lattika et al. (2025) found that individuals with more severe social anxiety symptoms tended to trust and embrace AI chatbots more readily, valuing their perceived non-judgemental support and emotional responsiveness; chatbot use was most strongly associated with younger age and psychological distress. Despite these findings, the existing literature has several important limitations. Much of the research is cross-sectional and survey based, limiting the ability to draw conclusions about causality. There is a lack of qualitative and longitudinal research exploring users' motivations, experiences and meaning making processes in relation to AI chatbot use. Much of the existing research has been conducted in Asian populations, with comparatively little work focusing on Western contexts. Studies often examine general chatbot use rather than distinguishing between therapeutic chatbots designed to reduce anxiety and non-therapeutic personal companion or social chatbots intended for companionship or intimacy. From a clinical perspective, it remains unclear whether engagement with personal companion AI chatbots may support or hinder recovery from social anxiety over time. Exposure-based approaches, which involve gradual engagement with feared social situations, are central to cognitive behavioural treatments for social anxiety and are recommended in clinical guidelines. It is therefore possible that chatbot interactions could function as a form of social skills rehearsal, emotional practice, or support that facilitates reduced anxiety in human interactions. Conversely, reliance on AI companionship may reinforce avoidance of real world social situations, potentially maintaining or exacerbating social anxiety. The disembodied disconnect hypothesis (Riva et al., 2024) suggests that digital social technologies may worsen anxiety and erode social skills for lonely or socially anxious users by reducing engagement in embodied, synchronised social interactions. In contrast, chatbot use as a supplement to human interaction - for example, as a tool for practice, advice or short-term support - may have more positive long term outcomes. Clinicians and researchers therefore face growing uncertainty about how to conceptualise emotionally meaningful relationships with AI chatbots: whether they represent adapting coping strategies for loneliness and anxiety, or whether they carry risks for increased withdrawal, loneliness and mental health difficulties (Ho et al., 2025). At the same time, AI development has often progressed without sufficient consideration of psychological theory or the potential impacts on social wellbeing and human relationships (Ventura et al., 2025). The purpose of this scoping review is to systematically map and synthesise the existing literature examining the relationship between personal companion AI chatbot use and social anxiety. Specifically, the review will explore: 1) what is currently known about associations between chatbot use and social anxiety, 2) proposed or examined mechanisms of impact, such as social skills rehearsal, emotional support, rumination or avoidance, 3) evidence regarding the duration and stability of any observed effects, and 4) distinctions between therapeutic and non-therapeutic AI chatbots. By identifying key findings, gaps and methodological trends in the literature, this scoping review aims to inform future research directions, clinical understanding and the development of psychologically informed AI technologies.
