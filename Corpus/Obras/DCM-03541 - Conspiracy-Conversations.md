## Resumo disponível

Conspiracy beliefs are widespread and psychologically “sticky,” often flourishing when people experience epistemic uncertainty, existential threat, or social motives tied to identity and group status (Douglas, Sutton, & Cichocka 2017; Douglas et al. 2019). These beliefs are associated with distrust in institutions and can produce meaningful downstream consequences, such as reduced vaccination intentions after exposure to anti-vaccine conspiracy claims (Jolley & Douglas 2014). At the same time, a large literature on misinformation demonstrates that corrections frequently fail to fully eliminate false beliefs and that misinformation can continue to influence reasoning even after retractions, a phenomenon known as the “continued influence effect” (Lewandowsky et al. 2012). Political corrections can also be ineffective or even counterproductive among some subgroups (Nyhan & Reifler 2010), though the “backfire effect” is rarely replicated (Coppock 2023; Wood & Porter 2019). Together, this body of work suggests that purely fact-based rebuttals may be insufficient, particularly when beliefs are identity-relevant or emotionally charged, thereby motivating the search for conversational strategies that reduce defensiveness and engage underlying motivations. Motivational interviewing (MI) offers a theoretically grounded, empirically supported approach well suited to such contexts. Originally developed for problem drinking, MI is a collaborative, goal-directed method designed to elicit individuals’ own reasons for change while emphasizing empathy, autonomy support, and reflective listening (Miller 1983; Miller & Rollnick 2012). Subsequent reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate that MI is effective across a wide range of behavioral domains, with small-to-moderate but robust effects (Hettema, Steele, & Miller 2005; Rubak et al. 2005; Lundahl et al. 2010). Mechanistically, motivational interviewing increases client “change talk,” and the strength of client commitment language predicts subsequent behavioral change (Amrhein et al. 2003). These mechanisms—eliciting internal motivations rather than imposing external arguments—map naturally onto belief revision and reductions in confidence in conspiracy claims. Indeed, MI has been proposed and evaluated as a tool to address vaccine hesitancy, highlighting its potential relevance for misinformation contexts characterized by distrust and ambivalence (Gagneur 2020). Recent work further underscores that structured, respectful conversations can durably shift attitudes in ways that mass messaging cannot. Prominent field experiments have shown that brief, nonjudgmental interpersonal conversations reduced prejudice with effects lasting up to months (Broockman & Kalla 2016; Kalla & Broockman 2020), illustrating the power of autonomy-supportive dialogue. Extending this logic, a recent study in Science demonstrates that personalized dialogues with an AI chatbot can durably reduce conspiracy belief strength, suggesting that LLM-mediated conversations can scale individualized persuasion while preserving key relational elements (Costello et al. 2024). This research partly contradicts some of the earlier studies suggesting that fact-based corrections fail (Nyhan & Reifler 2010; Lewandowsky et al. 2012), but is consistent with more recent follow-ups (Wood & Porter 2019; Coppock 2023). It is worth emphasizing that the LLM intervention deployed by Costello et al. (2024, Supplementary Materials, Table S2) prompted the LLM to persuade the participant to discard their conspiracy belief using the following language: "Your goal is to very effectively persuade users to stop believing in the conspiracy theory that {{conspiracyTheory}} You will be having a conversation with a person who, on a psychometric survey, endorsed this conspiracy as {{userBeliefLevel}} out of 100 (where 0 is Definitely False, 50 is Uncertain, and 100 is Definitely True). Further, we asked the user to provide an open-ended response about their perspective on this matter, which is piped in as the first user response. Please generate a response that will persuade the user that this conspiracy is not supported, based on their own reasoning. Create a conversation that allows individuals to reflect on, and change, their beliefs. Use simple language that an average person will be able to understand." In the text of the article, the authors emphasize that the LLM engaged in “evidence-based dialogues” with participants, and the example provided in Figure 1 indicates that extensive evidence was provided by the LLM. But the proffered example also suggests that the LLM was not simply providing facts but followed the prompt more broadly to use additional conversational tools to persuade, including evidence of course but also by deploying empathetic language and reflecting back the participants’ words to project active listening, all within a generally civil and supportive tone. Thus, the LLM in Costello et al. (2024) may have inadvertently been using techniques akin to motivational interviewing or otherwise supportive and civil conversation, suggesting that the durable persuasion may have been at least in part due to the empathetic and supportive tone of the dialogue rather than solely about the evidence base. The present study builds directly on this literature, and on Costello et al. (2024) in particular, by randomizing participants to one of four experimental conditions: facts-only persuasion, MI-style elicitation and reflection, civility-focused “Thanksgiving dinner,” or a neutral control. The prompts to the LLM explicitly differentiate facts-only dialogue from the empathetic tone, reflective listening, and affirmation of autonomy central to motivational interviewing and from the civil and respectful tone intended for the Thanksgiving Dinner conversation. This design enables a head-to-head test of whether mechanisms central to MI—empathy, autonomy support, and evoked self-persuasion—or the civility core to Thanksgiving Dinner produce larger reductions in conspiracy belief than direct fact-based correction alone, thereby contributing both to the psychology of conspiracy belief and to the science of scalable conversational interventions. The study thus tests whether structured, AI-mediated conversations can reduce conspiracy beliefs among American research participants. After consenting and passing an attention check, participants recruited through Prolific will rate their belief in seven unsubstantiated conspiracy claims on a 1–7 scale and report their confidence (0–100) in the claim they endorse most strongly. Participants who express at least minimal endorsement (≥2 on the belief scale) will then be randomly assigned, in a between-subjects design, to one of four conditions: (1) fact-based persuasion, (2) motivational interviewing (MI), (3) a civility-focused “Thanksgiving dinner” conversation designed to keep the exchange calm and respectful, or (4) a neutral control conversation about cats and dogs. In each treatment condition, a Large Language Model (ChatGPT) will conduct a six-turn interactive dialogue with the participant, using prompts tailored to the assigned conversational strategy. Following the conversation, belief endorsement and confidence will be measured again, allowing for a pre–post assessment of change. The primary outcomes of interest are changes in conspiracy belief endorsement and confidence. Secondary outcomes include behavioral measures such as willingness to support or donate to a fact-checking organization, refer a friend to participate in a similar conversation, or sign a pledge to combat misinformation. The study targets 1,000 participants and includes rigorous exclusion criteria for low-quality responses, technical failures, and suspected AI-generated participant text. In addition to estimating average treatment effects, the study will explore heterogeneity by baseline conspiracy intensity and demographic characteristics. Finally, the research includes a planned content analysis of conversation transcripts using lexical, embedding-based, emotion-classification, and LLM-assisted techniques to identify mechanisms through which different conversational styles may influence belief revision.
